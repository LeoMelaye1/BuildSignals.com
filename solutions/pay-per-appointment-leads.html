What you are actually buying

Most lead sources sell you a name and a phone number. What happens next is your problem. Some never pick up, some are renters, some wanted a price for a job they will never do. A pay per appointment program sells you the step after that: a homeowner who has already talked to a person, said yes to the project, confirmed a budget and picked a time for you to come out.

That difference matters most for small and mid-size remodelers. If you or one sales rep run every estimate, the hours you lose chasing dead leads are hours you are not in a home closing a job. Paying per appointment moves the chasing and the qualifying to someone else and lets you spend your time where the money is.

What counts as a qualified appointment

This is the part to get in writing before you sign with anyone. A vague definition is how contractors end up paying for appointments that were never real. At BuildSignals a billable appointment is:

  • A homeowner. Not a renter, and not a tenant asking on behalf of a landlord.
  • A real project. They want the work done, not just a ballpark number for later.
  • Budget confirmed. They confirmed a budget on the phone before we booked you.
  • In your service area and on your calendar. An in-home estimate at a time that works for you.
  • Confirmed before the visit. We reach out again before you drive over.

How to tell if a per-appointment price makes sense for you

You do not need anyone's pitch to answer this. You need three numbers from your own business:

  1. Your average gross profit on a job of the type you want more of (tub-to-shower, full bath, kitchen).
  2. Your close rate on in-home estimates that were actually qualified. Be honest; count the last 20 or so.
  3. How much you are willing to spend to win one job.

Multiply your average gross profit per job by your close rate. That is roughly what one qualified appointment is worth to you. Then decide what share of that you are willing to spend on marketing. If you close one in three, each appointment is worth about a third of your profit on a job. If the per-appointment price plus your share of ad spend sits well under that, the program pays for itself. If your close rate swings a lot, pay per close may fit you better.

One outside note on timing: Harvard's Joint Center for Housing Studies projects that homeowner spending on improvements will reach about $518 billion by the end of 2026, with growth easing late in the year (LIRA, revised April 2026). A slower market is when wasted estimates hurt the most, and when paying only for qualified ones helps the most.

Who pay per appointment fits best

  • Kitchen and bath remodelers who close a solid share of the estimates they run.
  • Owners who run their own sales and cannot afford to spend evenings calling leads back.
  • Companies with crew capacity that want a steady, predictable number of estimates each week.
  • Contractors burned by retainers or shared leads who want to pay for results instead.

It is a weaker fit if you are not able to run estimates within a few days of booking, or if you do not want to take in-home appointments at all.
