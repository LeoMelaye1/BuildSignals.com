What Counts as a Qualified Remodeling Appointment?

By Leo Melaye, founder of BuildSignals · November 24, 2026

A qualified remodeling appointment is an in-home estimate with a homeowner who owns the house, has a real project you do, has a budget that fits your prices, wants to start within a reasonable window, will have every decision maker at the table, and has confirmed the visit. If any one of those is missing, you have a lead with a time slot, not a qualified appointment.

That definition matters more than it sounds. If you buy appointments from a lead company, the word "qualified" decides what you pay for. If you book your own, it decides how your salespeople spend their week. Below is a plain checklist you can use for both, plus the gray areas that cause most arguments.

Why the word "qualified" causes so many fights

Most remodelers have had this conversation. A vendor says they booked 12 appointments. The sales team says only 5 were real. Both are telling the truth, because they are using different definitions.

The vendor counts a name, a phone number and a time on the calendar. The salesperson counts a homeowner who was home, wanted the job, could afford it and could say yes. The gap between those two numbers is where money disappears. Writing the definition down before the first appointment is booked fixes most of it.

The 7-point checklist for a qualified appointment

Use these as yes or no questions. A qualified appointment gets a yes on all seven.

  1. They own the home. Renters and landlords' tenants cannot sign for a bathroom remodel. Ask directly: "Do you own the home?" If it is a family member's house, the owner needs to be part of the visit.
  2. It is a project you actually do. A tub-to-shower conversion is a fit for a bath remodeler. A leaky faucet is not. Confirm the scope in one sentence before booking.
  3. The home is in your service area. Check the ZIP code or city, not just the state. A lead 70 miles past your last job is a cost, not an opportunity.
  4. The budget fits your work. You do not need an exact number. You need to know the homeowner expects a price in your range. A simple range question works: "Most full bathroom projects we do land between X and Y. Is that in the range you were planning?"
  5. The timing is real. Someone planning to remodel "in a couple of years" is a nurture lead. Someone who wants it done in the next few months is an appointment.
  6. All decision makers will be there. If two people own the house and both will decide, both should be at the estimate. Ask: "Is there anyone else who will be part of this decision?"
  7. The visit is confirmed. A booked time is not a confirmed time. The homeowner should confirm by text or phone before the visit, ideally the day before and again that morning.

Qualified appointment vs. lead vs. sat appointment

These three terms get mixed up constantly. Here is how they differ.

TermWhat it meansWhat you still have to do
LeadA homeowner filled out a form or called. Contact info and a project interest.Call fast, qualify, book, confirm, show up, sell.
Booked appointmentA time is on your calendar. Qualification may or may not have happened.Check the details, confirm, show up, sell.
Qualified appointmentBooked, and the homeowner passed the 7-point checklist.Show up and sell.
Sat (or shown) appointmentYour salesperson actually met the homeowner and presented.Close or follow up.

The most honest unit is the sat appointment, because it is the only one that proves the homeowner showed up. Everything before it is a promise. That is why it is worth asking any vendor whether they charge for booked appointments or for appointments that actually happened. The gray areas (and how to decide them up front)

Most disputes come from a short list of situations. Decide each one in writing before you start.

Only one spouse shows up

Some companies treat this as unqualified and reschedule. Others run the appointment anyway, because plenty of homeowners make the decision alone. Pick a rule. A common middle ground: it counts if the person at the table said during booking that they make the decision, and it does not count if they said their spouse needed to be there and the spouse did not come.

The homeowner wants a smaller job than they said

Someone books a full bathroom remodel, and at the visit they only want a new vanity. If the booking notes show the larger scope, the appointment was qualified when it was booked. Scope often shrinks once people see prices. That is a sales conversation, not a qualification failure.

The budget was "fine" on the phone, then not fine at the table

This usually means the budget question was too vague. "Do you have a budget?" gets a yes from everyone. A range question with real numbers from your own past jobs gets an honest answer. Fix the question rather than arguing about the appointment.

No-shows and same-day cancellations

A no-show is not a sat appointment, and it should not be billed as one. Whether a reschedule counts as a new appointment is worth deciding ahead of time. Our guide on reducing no-shows for in-home remodeling estimates covers the confirmation steps that prevent most of them.

Out-of-area addresses

These should be caught before booking, not at the door. If someone slips through, the fair answer is that it does not count.

How to write your own definition

Whether you book appointments in-house or pay someone to do it, put your definition on one page. Keep it short enough that the person making calls can follow it from memory.

  • Services you sell: list them by name (tub-to-shower, walk-in shower, full bath remodel, kitchen remodel).
  • Service area: a ZIP code list or a map with a drive-time limit.
  • Budget floor: the lowest job size you want to run an estimate for, stated as a range question your caller asks.
  • Timing window: for example, wants to start within the next six months.
  • Decision makers: your rule for spouses and co-owners.
  • Confirmation steps: when and how the visit is confirmed.
  • What does not count: renters, out-of-area, no-shows, wrong service, duplicates of a lead you already had.

Then track it. For every appointment, mark whether it passed each point and whether it sat. After a month you will see which question is letting bad appointments through. Usually it is budget or decision makers.

Questions to ask a lead company about "qualified"

If you pay for appointments, ask these before you sign:

  • What exactly do you check before booking? Can I see the questions your callers ask?
  • Do you charge for booked appointments, or only ones that sit?
  • What happens if the homeowner turns out to be a renter or outside my area?
  • Who confirms the appointment, and when?
  • Are the leads exclusive to me, or sold to other contractors too?
  • Can I see every lead, not just the ones that booked?

A vendor with a clear written answer to each of these is usually easier to work with than one that only quotes a price. If you are weighing shared and exclusive sources, our comparison of exclusive vs. shared remodeling leads goes deeper.

Quick answers

What is a qualified appointment in home improvement?

An in-home estimate with a homeowner who owns the home, has a project you do, is in your service area, has a budget that fits your prices, plans to start soon, will have every decision maker present, and has confirmed the visit.

What is the difference between a booked appointment and a sat appointment?

A booked appointment is a time on your calendar. A sat (or shown) appointment means your salesperson actually met the homeowner and presented. Only a sat appointment proves the homeowner showed up.

Should an appointment count if only one spouse is there?

Decide it in writing before you start. A common rule: it counts if the person said at booking that they make the decision alone, and it does not count if they said their spouse had to be there and the spouse did not come.

Should I pay for no-show appointments?

No. A no-show is not a sat appointment. Ask any lead company up front whether they charge for booked appointments or only for appointments that actually happen.
